Money, utility functions, and equality

Money, utility functions, and equality

Jul 03, 20268 min read

  • Wealth inequality is a function of:
    • Homogenous utility functions
    • and a critical underproduction of a single good
  • Wealth equality is a function of:
    • Heterogenous utility functions
    • No critical underproduction of a single good

Thus wealth equality would seem like a good thing. So why does optimizing it lead to poverty? It seems to optimizing it leads to underproduction of all goods. Likely because the easiest way to improve wealth equality is actually by critically underproducing all goods rather than increasing the diversity of utility functions.

The thing to care about is producing contrarians. Contrarians increase diversity of utility functions and create value.

If you’re a capitalist, you ought to worry about accidentally homogenizing people’s utility functions. If you’re a politician you ought to worry about accidentally making everyone poor by stopping the rich from producing critical goods.

I think the standard, maybe Silicon Valley conception of money is something along the lines of: “This money is a representation of value.” Maybe slightly more formal would be: “When you put labor into delivering value to people, they pay you money when they perceive that you’re going to deliver value to them.” You can do this at scale, and then you acquire a lot of money, which acts as an IOU on society that you are owed a certain amount of labor. In that sense, money and wealth are broadly really good measures of how much value one is providing to society.

I think this framework slightly misses the mark specifically on illegitimate methods of acquiring money. Maybe if you were optimizing for money, you would find that a faster and cheaper way to acquire money is to steal it from people. This is maybe considered a reward hack. It’s not what is intended when we incentivize society to go and acquire money. Society effectively deems this as illegitimate acquisition of money. If you do it that way and get caught, we take the money away from you because you clearly did not provide value. You’ve even destroyed value and yet you have acquired money.

I think there’s a gray area between this very obvious reward hack and something that’s in the middle. I think much of sin industries or goods and products that are addicting fall into this category. An example could be drugs or casinos. We have value uncertainty around drugs. Is someone purchasing drugs because you’re actually delivering value to them, or is it because you’ve somehow tricked their nervous system and their biochem, chemical, neurological makeup to basically convince them to keep coming back to you to give you money for something that actually isn’t providing the value? The second point is kind of like: how do we define money and property rights in the face of such a thing? I think money is socially defined. I think the main argument one can make for Bitcoin is we have extremely high uncertainty about the illicit methods by which someone can acquire money. We have extremely high value uncertainty, and therefore, broadly, any method by which you acquire this goes. We try to codify it in code, and if you reward hack it, then you get to keep it. I think this is effectively the code is law concept.
