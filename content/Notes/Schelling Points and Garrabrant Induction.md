Richard Ngo has recently been pointing at an interesting problem. How do we formally model Schelling points? It's his claim (I haven't verified) that Schelling points broadly lack formalism.

There seems to be something deep underneath the desire to formalize this. Schelling points are a game theoretic concept underneath a number of rational abnormalities as well as coordination failures in the social sciences.

It seems it would be difficult to model:
- market reflexivity
- economic booms and busts
- long-term planning
- optimal political philosophy
- choosing money designs
- why do certain rights matter more
- multi-agent equillibria
- ultimately AI alignment
- nuclear non proliferation

The core of what's being pointed at is that schelling points are solution to an infinite set of game theoretic equilibria. The classic example is finding a place to choose to meet up in NYC with a friend on a Tuesday. The options include time square, penn station, 5th ave, east village, brooklyn store house, etc. And you have 24 hours in a day. Any option is equally correct to the others, so why choose Times Square at Midnight, or grand central at noon? And how exactly does that process come about?

There are two players modeling each other with infinite regress. I think about what you think I'm thinking about you .......

Potentially a ***friction*** imposed to this infinite regress is that rationality is actually **bounded.** It is not that humans are not rational, but rather that we have a limited amount of computation available to us. There are only so many steps I'm able to reason through the chain. I'll apply some kind of bayesian prior and update a few times over. I might know that you don't overthinkg things and you might know that I chronically overthink my models of others. 

So I'll assume you'll choose the first location and time that comes to your mind and that you won't change your mind so that I can model you appropriately.

We both have a limited amount of compute available to us and we kind of know what we spend that compute on. This **boundedness*** perhaps introduces frictions to the equillibria options such that there is only realistically 1 option to choose from -> the Schelling point.

Maybe greater future wealth is downstream of increasing the amount of computation.
