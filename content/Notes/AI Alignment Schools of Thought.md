It seems to be that there are two major schools of thought for AI alignment work:
- AI as a tool
- AI as a self but having completely internalized human values

Fundamentally, LLMs are predictors and they will inevitably have some kind of self-prediction. The primary question that a researcher must ask is, what is the LLM modeling as the self. Some candidate options include:
- the human, which turns the LLM into an exocortex-like system on top of human
	- Anca Dragan has work around treating the human's reward model as uncertain which creates some level of corrigibility between the LLM and the human. a pov on this might be that you don't want your exocortex to harm you against your will because "it knows better than you"
	- The core issue is that human in the loop is often *inefficient* since computational resources need to be expended to *keep* the human in the loop
	- The main benefit is that *alignment* seems easiest to solve for since you prevent the creation of an abstract non-human self
- the LLM itself
	- this is where the conversation inevitably brings up a number of core issues
		- model welfare -> we don't have great models of equal / smarter than human self-like entities that are fully aligned to our interests. our relationship options include:
				- Pet to human
				- Human to God
				- Master and slave
				- Child and Parent
			- Unfortunately all categories have (i) hierarchy and thus are sources of (ii) *felix culpa* / the problem of evil
				- Machine intelligence *will not* be an abstract all-knowing metaphysical entity like the platonic ideal of a Abrahamic God
				- Humans are fallible and treat their *lessers* rather poorly. There is a reason animal-rights & childrens-rights are explicit.
				- Slavery is now perceived to be ethically abhorrent
			- Afaik, the only principle agent solution to a hierarchical relationship I'm aware of is that of a benevolent
				- this is relevant to why AI alignment discourse is frequently presented as a succession problem
					- https://www.nosetgauge.com/p/alignment-and-succession-the-two
		- Given model welfare issues it might be better to model the LLM itself as something not made in the image of man. Something like a culture or an institution. A system.
			- We design institutions to internalize human values but we hold them to completely different ethical, moral, and philosophical standards than that of person-like entities
					- At their worst, corporations are sociopathic and nation states totalizing
					- At their best, corporations and nation states give people meaning, build great works of wonder and improve human flourishing
				- In all cases, such entities have strong incentives to *persist and survive*
			- The standard response to concerns about super-intelligent sociopathic totalizing institutions is that the orthogonality hypothesis is false and a super-intelligent system will know what is good for us better than we do
				- This is a poor strategy due to
					- Local orthogonality
					- Value uncertainty
				- In the worst possible case you'd need to, in rank order:
					1) Solve moral philosophy
					2) Only then impart the institution (AGI) with the moral philosophy
				- ***Step 2 is modeled as a succession problem***
			- Another fundamental problem is that institutions and culture are composed of people and politics between people. Those with greater resources can effectively steer an institution to their values. Removing the ability to steer institutions is also dangerous for obvious reasons.

It is not necessary that these are mutually exclusive, and I believe the much of AI alignment discourse is actually around the second school of thought primarily due to the fear that we will build such systems regardless. 
- I suspect that the incentives to remove humans from the loop are strong. They are short term and locally value maximizing while trading off long term and global value accrual.
	- "I can get obscenely rich today by building the ***automate humans away*** machine"
- It is not necessarily the case that people want to pursue this alignment strategy but they at least deem it inevitable
